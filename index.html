How secure is energy across Africa?

The Africa Energy Security Index tracks whether households, firms and governments can count on reliable, accessible and affordable energy, measured the same way for every country across five dimensions. It covers 27 Sub-Saharan African countries from 2005 to 2023.

What the index measures

Energy security is more than having enough supply. The five dimensions are energy system adequacy (System), access and affordability (Access), market and external vulnerability (Market), environmental sustainability (Environment), and the political-institutional environment (Institutions). The index combines 21 indicators across these five dimensions so that a country's score reflects the whole picture, from generation capacity to what a household pays for electricity.

System

Whether the energy system reliably delivers energy, including grid losses and energy intensity.

Access

Electricity and clean cooking access, and electricity use per person.

Market

Import and fossil-fuel dependence, and exposure to world oil price shocks.

Environment

Emissions, emissions intensity and the share of renewables.

Institutions

Political stability, government effectiveness, rule of law and control of corruption.

Concept

Energy security is commonly organised around the "4 As": availability, accessibility, affordability and acceptability. The framework is simple, but a growing body of research questions how well it works for risk-based, system-level analysis, particularly in emerging and developing economies.

Four lines of critique shape the design of this index. First, energy security is multidimensional and context-specific, so a fixed checklist of attributes cannot capture it fully. Second, attribute-based frameworks are static: they say little about how well an energy system actually delivers, or about the institutional and socioeconomic conditions it operates in. Third, energy security can be seen as a systemic risk condition in which policy goals often conflict, for example when expanding rural energy access works against emissions reduction. Fourth, energy security is increasingly measured as exposure to risk rather than as the presence of particular attributes.

Two weaknesses of the 4 As matter especially here. "Acceptability" is normatively defined and hard to measure, and the framework largely leaves out the political and institutional conditions that reliable supply and long-term investment depend on. The index therefore uses a risk-based, five-dimension structure suited to the structural and persistent energy challenges of Sub-Saharan Africa. It keeps environmental sustainability and institutions as separate dimensions, so each can be tracked on its own.

Five dimensions

The index has five dimensions: energy system adequacy (System), access and affordability (Access), market and external vulnerability (Market), environmental sustainability (Environment), and the political-institutional environment (Institutions). Indicators

Each dimension is measured by three to seven indicators. Indicators marked − describe vulnerability or environmental harm and are reversed (multiplied by −1) before standardisation, so that within every dimension a higher value always means more secure. Indicators marked + enter as they are.

Data and preparation

All indicators come from three free, standardised World Bank sources that are updated every year: the World Development Indicators for energy, emissions, trade and macroeconomic series; the Worldwide Governance Indicators for the four governance measures; and the Commodity Markets "Pink Sheet" for the Brent crude oil price. The oil price is not country-specific, so it is combined with each country's trade openness and fuel-import share to build the two oil-shock exposure measures.

Panels were built dimension by dimension: for each dimension, the relevant series were extracted for all candidate countries and merged into a country-year panel. Gaps in the raw data are filled using the least invasive method that works, in this order:

  1. Linear interpolation within a country for gaps between observed years.
  2. A three-year centred moving average for remaining short gaps.
  3. Forward filling for gaps at the end of a series.
  4. Backward filling, then the country mean and, only where necessary, the sample mean.

This keeps country-years in the panel rather than dropping them because one indicator is missing. A country is excluded only when a whole dimension cannot be computed for it.

Normalisation

Indicators are measured in very different units, from kilowatt-hours per person to governance scores between −2.5 and 2.5, so they are placed on a common scale before they are combined. Two steps are applied to every indicator. First, indicators that move in the less secure direction (transmission losses, fossil-fuel dependence, emissions and import exposure) are multiplied by −1, so that a higher value always means greater security. Second, each indicator is standardised. Three techniques are applied in parallel: the z-score is the main specification, and min–max and softmax are robustness checks.

Z-score (main)Zi = (Xi − X̄) / σ
Min–maxmmxi = (Xi − Xmin) / (Xmax − Xmin)
Softmaxsi = 1 / (1 + e−Vi),   Vi = (Xi − X̄) / σ

Softmax compresses the pull of extreme values without removing them, which matters for indicators such as total emissions and oil-shock exposure that are highly skewed across countries. Outliers are handled through the choice of normalisation technique itself, with no further transformation. Z-score and min–max are the conventional choices and allow comparison with earlier studies; softmax tests whether conclusions are driven by a few outlying country-years. Softmax is applied within each country's own time series.

Building the index

Rather than putting every indicator into a single principal component analysis (PCA), the index is built in two stages. This makes it possible to see which dimensions drive a country's overall score.

  1. Stage 1: one index per dimension

    For each of the five dimensions, PCA is run on that dimension's standardised indicators. The first principal component, which captures the largest share of their common variation, becomes the dimension index. Before each PCA, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure and Bartlett's test of sphericity check that the indicators are correlated enough for PCA to be appropriate.

    zk = αk⊤X,   Var(zk) = λk,   k = 1, …, K

  2. Rescale each dimension to 0–1

    Each dimension index is rescaled with min–max so that 0 is the least secure and 1 the most secure observation on that dimension.

  3. Stage 2: combine the five dimensions

    A second PCA is run on the five dimension indices. Its first principal component is the composite Energy Security Index, and its loadings act as data-driven weights for each dimension.

    ESI = a1·System + a2·Access + a3·Market + a4·Environment + a5·Institutions

  4. Rescale and rank

    The composite is rescaled to 0–1 and countries are ranked by it.

Reading the scores

Every published score, both the composite and the five dimension indices, runs from 0 to 1. Values closer to 1 mean greater energy security; values closer to 0 mean less. Because each dimension is reported alongside the composite, two countries with similar overall scores can be compared on how they reach them.

Scores are relative to the countries and years in the panel, so they show a country's position and trajectory compared with its peers rather than an absolute threshold of security.

Country coverage

The index covers 27 Sub-Saharan African countries observed every year from 2005 to 2023, a balanced panel of 513 country-year observations. The World Bank classifies 48 countries as Sub-Saharan Africa, so the index covers 56% of the region's economies.

Angola, Benin, Botswana, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Congo (Dem. Rep.), Congo (Rep.), Côte d'Ivoire, Eswatini, Ethiopia, Gabon, Ghana, Kenya, Madagascar, Mauritius, Mozambique, Namibia, Niger, Nigeria, Rwanda, Senegal, South Africa, Sudan, Tanzania, Togo, Uganda and Zimbabwe.

Countries were selected on data availability rather than by a predetermined sampling design. A country is included only if the World Development Indicators and Worldwide Governance Indicators provide continuous enough coverage of every indicator in all five dimensions over 2005–2023. Most of the countries left out are small island states, fragile and conflict-affected states whose statistical systems were disrupted for long periods, and other countries with sparse data over this period.

The 27 countries still span net energy exporters, diversified middle-income economies and energy-poor, import-dependent economies, so the index captures the wide range of energy security situations in the region.
